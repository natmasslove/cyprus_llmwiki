# Overview

The Tombs of the Kings is a large necropolis located approximately 2 kilometres north of Paphos harbour in Cyprus. The underground tombs are carved out of solid rock and date primarily from the 4th century BC to the 3rd century AD. Despite the name, no kings were actually buried here; the site served as the burial ground for Paphian aristocrats and high officials. In 1980, the site was designated a UNESCO World Heritage Site along with Nea Paphos and Kouklia.

# History

The tombs have been known and explored for centuries, with the oldest modern account written by Richard Pockocke in 1783. The first archaeological excavations were conducted in 1870 by Luigi Palma di Cesnola, the American consul to Cyprus. Scientific excavations began in 1915 under Menelaos Markides, curator of the Cyprus Museum, with systematic work continuing in the late 1970s and 1980s under Sophocles Hadjisavvas. Much information about the tombs has been lost over time due to grave robbing and the proximity to the seaside, which hindered preservation of buried bodies.

# What to see

The tombs are cut into native rock and often imitate the architecture of houses of the living. Some tombs feature impressive Doric columns and frescoed walls, reflecting the magnificence that gave the site its royal name. Archaeological excavations continue at the site, revealing connections to the Ptolemaic dynasty, including evidence related to the burial of the last king of Cyprus who committed suicide after Roman occupation. The site is particularly important for dating archaeological material through Rhodian amphorae found among burial offerings, which bear manufacturing stamps on their handles.

# Location & access

The necropolis is situated about 2 kilometres north of Paphos harbour on the western coast of Cyprus. The tombs are carved into the solid rock of the coastal terrain in the Paphos region.
